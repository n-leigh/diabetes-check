Case Study Background

A Web-Based Diabetes
Complication Prediction System

Developed as a case study for a Professional Elective on Data Analytics, focused on applying classification models to a real-world healthcare screening problem.

Study Background

Diabetes complications — including cardiovascular disease, neuropathy, and broader health burden — are a leading driver of long-term morbidity among people living with diabetes. Early, low-cost screening from routinely available health indicators can help flag elevated risk before it's confirmed through more expensive clinical testing.

DiaBeates was built to explore whether that screening can be done two ways at once: a transparent, clinically-cited rule matrix that scores known risk factors against established thresholds, and a trained classification model that learns the same patterns directly from real patient data — shown side by side rather than choosing one over the other.

The system is trained on the CDC Diabetes Health Indicators dataset (BRFSS 2015), filtered to 35,346 diabetic-positive respondents, and evaluates risk across three categories: cardiovascular complications, neuropathy/mobility impact, and general complication burden. An optional lab-values layer (HbA1c, blood pressure, LDL cholesterol) adds a more precise, clinically-cited assessment when real lab results are available.

Rule Matrix

Clinically-Cited Scoring

Encodes established risk factors (hypertension, cholesterol, smoking, mobility difficulty, general health burden) into a transparent point system, cited to ADA Standards of Care and NHANES threshold research. Every score is explainable — no black box.

Classification Models

Trained & Compared

Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, and Random Forest classifiers are each trained per complication category; the best-performing model on held-out data is kept and shown alongside the rule matrix's result.

Ethics, Limitations & Disclaimer

Not a diagnostic tool

DiaBeates is an academic case study built to demonstrate classification modeling on a healthcare screening problem — it is not a certified or clinically validated medical device. Risk levels shown are statistical estimates, not diagnoses. Results should never replace evaluation by a licensed healthcare provider, and no treatment decision should be made on this tool's output alone.

Ground truth comes from rules, not diagnosed outcomes

The classifiers were trained on labels generated by our own rule matrix, not on confirmed clinical diagnoses of complications. This means the model's job is to learn to approximate the rule matrix efficiently — not to discover new medical patterns. Two of the three categories reach near-perfect accuracy for exactly this reason: their underlying rules are simple enough that a classifier can reconstruct them almost exactly. This is expected and disclosed here rather than presented as a stronger result than it is.

Dataset scope

Training data comes from the CDC's BRFSS survey — self-reported health data from U.S. respondents, not lab-confirmed clinical records. Findings may not generalize to populations, healthcare systems, or reporting norms outside that context. The optional lab-values layer (HbA1c, blood pressure, LDL) is scored against cited clinical thresholds, but has not itself been clinically validated as a predictive tool.

Data handling

Assessments entered into this system are stored locally in the app's own database for demonstration purposes (e.g. the History page). No real patient data, consent process, or production-grade privacy safeguards are involved — this system is not intended for deployment with real patient records without further ethical review and safeguards.
